## 1. Queue-position and fill-probability models (queue-reactive, depletion/cancellation, survival/hazard, L2 queue-position estimators)

### Takeaway
With only L2 data, the standard practical tool is a **probabilistic queue-position tracker**: you start at the back of the queue, trades at your price always move you forward, and size decreases that are not trades (cancellations) are split between "ahead of you" and "behind you" by a probability function of (front, back). Bracket it between a pessimistic model (cancels always behind you) and an optimistic one. Then calibrate it, together with a censored survival or hazard model of time-to-fill (20 s horizon), on your own live fills. The theory (Cont–Stoikov–Talreja; Cont–de Larrard; Huang–Lehalle–Rosenbaum) says fill probability inside a fixed horizon depends mainly on: queue ahead, the depletion rate of the level (taker volume plus cancels ahead of you), and the opposite queue size / imbalance. Moallemi–Yuan show that in large-tick books, queue position on its own can be worth about as much as the half-spread. That is consistent with the observed ~250-contract break-even.

### Cited Findings

**L2 queue-position estimators (hftbacktest, the practical reference implementation)**
- hftbacktest only supports Market-By-Price (L2) data for queue estimation: "If an exchange doesn't provide Market-By-Order, you have to guess it by modeling." It ships a conservative `RiskAverseQueueModel` and a family of `ProbQueueModel`s. — [hftbacktest docs, order_fill.rst](https://github.com/nkaz001/hftbacktest/blob/master/docs/order_fill.rst)
- **RiskAverseQueueModel:** "decrease in quantity by cancellation or modification ... happens only at the tail of the queue so your order queue position doesn't change. The order queue position will be advanced only if a trade happens at the price." In code: on a new order, front = current level qty; on a trade at the price, front -= trade_qty; on a depth update, front = min(front, new_qty). — [hftbacktest queue.rs](https://github.com/nkaz001/hftbacktest/blob/master/hftbacktest/src/backtest/models/queue.rs)
- **ProbQueueModel update rule** (from source). On a depth decrease, `chg = prev_qty - new_qty - cum_trade_qty`; the trade volume is subtracted so the same decrease is not counted twice. If chg < 0 (the level grew), the queue ahead is unchanged. Otherwise, with `back = prev_qty - front` and `p = prob(front, back)`: `est_front = front - (1-p)*chg + min(back - p*chg, 0)`, then `front = min(est_front, new_qty)`. Here p is the share of a cancel assumed to happen *behind* you. — [hftbacktest queue.rs](https://github.com/nkaz001/hftbacktest/blob/master/hftbacktest/src/backtest/models/queue.rs)
- **Probability functions provided:** PowerProbQueueFunc `p = f(back)/(f(back)+f(front))` with f(x)=x^n; LogProbQueueFunc, the same with f(x)=log(1+x); LogProbQueueFunc2 `f(back)/f(back+front)`; PowerProbQueueFunc2 `f(back)/f(back+front)`; PowerProbQueueFunc3 `1 - f(front/(front+back))`. Required boundary conditions: p=0 at the head of the queue (all decreases happen after you) and p=1 at the tail. The log form "exhibits a different probability profile depending on the total quantity at the price level, unlike power functions." — [hftbacktest docs](https://github.com/nkaz001/hftbacktest/blob/master/docs/order_fill.rst); [queue.rs](https://github.com/nkaz001/hftbacktest/blob/master/hftbacktest/src/backtest/models/queue.rs)
- The method traces back to rigtorp (2013), "Estimating order queue position", a quant.stackexchange thread (#3782), and Almgren's PIMS slides. — [hftbacktest docs references](https://github.com/nkaz001/hftbacktest/blob/master/docs/order_fill.rst); [rigtorp.se](https://rigtorp.se/2013/06/08/estimating-order-queue-position.html)
- **Fill rules in hftbacktest's default NoPartialFillExchange:** a resting buy fills if price >= best ask, *or* price > a sell trade price (the market traded through you), *or* you are at the front of the queue and price == sell trade price. The PartialFillExchange lets trades at your price partially fill you, by the remaining trade quantity, once you are at the front. The docs warn that replay cannot model your own market impact and that results must be checked against live trading. — [hftbacktest docs](https://github.com/nkaz001/hftbacktest/blob/master/docs/order_fill.rst)

**Queue-reactive models**
- Huang, Lehalle & Rosenbaum (2015, JASA; arXiv 1312.0563), "Simulating and analyzing order book data: the queue-reactive model". The arrival intensity of each event type (limit order, cancel, market order) at each level is a function of the current book state, chiefly that level's own queue size. Queue sizes are measured in units of mean event size (later work uses median event size). Empirical result on France Telecom: at the first limit, insertion intensity is roughly flat in queue size and lower when the queue is empty; at the second limit, insertion decreases with queue size. — [arXiv 1312.0563](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1312.0563); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259044843_Simulating_and_Analyzing_Order_Book_Data_The_Queue-Reactive_Model); [MES normalization, arXiv 2405.18594](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.18594)
- **[TK]** In the HLR estimates, the cancellation intensity at the best queue rises roughly linearly with queue size (cancels are roughly proportional to resting volume), and the market-order intensity falls as the queue grows. For a resting order, this means the cancels in a big queue are mostly a constant-per-order hazard. Some fall ahead of you, so a long queue thins faster than taker volume alone would suggest. — [arXiv 1312.0563](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.0563)
- Extensions: queue-reactive Hawkes models (arXiv 1901.08938); multi-dimensional QR and signal-driven models (arXiv 2506.11843); deep-learning QR (arXiv 2501.08822); "Detection of exogenous price moves: localising the QR model" (Lehalle 2024). — [1901.08938](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.08938); [2506.11843](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.11843); [2501.08822](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.08822); [Lehalle project page](http://www.cmap.polytechnique.fr/~charles-albert.lehalle/projects/2024QR/)

**Queue depletion / cancellation models**
- Cont, Stoikov & Talreja (2010, Operations Research 58(3):549–563): the book is a continuous-time Markov chain with Poisson limit orders, market orders and cancellations. Laplace-transform methods compute conditional probabilities given the book state: that the mid-price rises, that "an order at the bid executes before the ask quote moves", and that a buy and a sell both execute at the best quotes before the price moves. The model captures short-term dynamics on high-frequency data, and the paper evaluates a simple strategy built on it. — [CST 2010 PDF](http://rama.cont.perso.math.cnrs.fr/pdf/CST2010.pdf); [SSRN 1273160](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1273160)
- **[TK]** In CST, the cancellation rate at distance i is theta(i)·x (proportional to queue size x). The parameters are estimated from arrival counts. Market-order rate mu, limit-order arrival lambda(i) follows a power law in distance from the opposite best quote, and cancel rate theta(i) is per resting order. Those are exactly the inputs needed for a fill-before-price-move probability. — [CST 2010 PDF](http://rama.cont.perso.math.cnrs.fr/pdf/CST2010.pdf)
- **[TK]** Cont & de Larrard (2013, SIAM J. Financial Math; arXiv 1104.4596), "Price dynamics in a Markovian limit order market". This is a reduced model with only the best bid and ask queues, aimed at large-tick stocks. Queues change through Poisson order and cancel flow; when one queue depletes, the price moves one tick and new queues are drawn from a stationary distribution. Closed-form results: the probability that the next price move is up, given queue sizes (x at the bid, y at the ask), rises with x relative to y. The time to depletion has a heavy-tailed distribution when the order inflow rate equals the outflow rate (lambda = mu + theta). Price becomes diffusive at longer scales. — [arXiv 1104.4596](https://arxiv.org/abs/1104.4596)
- Guo, Ruan & Zhu (arXiv 1505.04810), "Dynamics of order positions and related queues in a LOB": fluid and diffusion limits for the position of a specific order within the queue, including how cancellations ahead of you advance your position. — [arXiv 1505.04810](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1505.04810)

**Queue-position valuation**
- Moallemi & Yuan (2016/2017; SSRN 2996221), "A model for queue position valuation in a LOB". Queue value = a static part (spread earned minus adverse-selection cost, *which rises with position in the queue*) plus a dynamic part (the option value of holding a queue spot). The model is calibrated on large-tick US stocks, where the spread is 1 tick and the queues are large. For some large-tick stocks, queue value is of the same order of magnitude as the bid-ask spread (a later citing paper says "half spread"). Predictions are validated against backtest estimates. — [SSRN 2996221](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2996221); [Columbia page](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/model-queue-position-valuation-limit-order-book); [2014 slides](http://market-microstructure.institutlouisbachelier.org/uploads/91_7%20MOALLEMI%202014-12-paris-mm-queue-value.pdf); [MIT abstract](https://web.mit.edu/orc/www/archive/seminars/2014s/abs/Moallemi_abs.html)
- Lehalle and coauthors' "From Glosten–Milgrom to the whole LOB" (arXiv 1902.10743) reports queue-position values at the best ask of the same order as the bid-ask spread, and uses the model to forecast the effect of a tick-size change. — [arXiv 1902.10743](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.10743)
- 2025 (J. Financial Markets, "Queuing and inventories in limit order markets"): orders further back in the queue fill against larger, better-informed trades. Empirically, one standard deviation more contracts ahead goes with fills about 20% smaller on average. — [ScienceDirect S1386418125000229](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1386418125000229)

**Survival / hazard models of execution**
- Lo, MacKinlay & Zhang (2002, JFE 65(1):31–71; NBER WP 6257, 1997), "Econometric models of limit-order executions". **[TK]** They fit generalized-gamma accelerated-failure-time survival models to actual limit-order execution times. Main results: execution time is very sensitive to limit price (distance from the quote) and to market conditions, but *not* to order size; and "first-passage-time" proxies (assuming you fill when the price touches your level) are a poor match for real execution times. — [NBER 6257](https://www.nber.org/papers/w6257); JFE citation via search
- Maglaras, Moallemi & Wang (Quantitative Finance 22(11):1989–2003, 2022): an RNN estimates the full time-to-fill *distribution* conditional on the current book. Trained on hypothetical fixed-price limit orders with an assumed zero impact; it gives meaningful cost reductions in a prototypical execution problem. — [Moallemi PDF](https://moallemi.com/ciamac/papers/deep-lob-2021.pdf); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-deep-learning-approach-to-estimating-fill-in-a-Maglaras-Moallemi/07ee2c98e9317a40ce20f28771f939302b41743e)
- Arroyo, Cartea, Moreno-Pino & Zohren (2023), "Deep attentive survival analysis in LOBs": a convolutional-Transformer with a monotonic network that guarantees a monotone survival function, trained on right-censored log-likelihood. They argue that AUC-ROC (used by earlier work) is an improper scoring rule for survival problems. KANFormer (arXiv 2512.05734, 2025) adds agent-level features. — [arXiv 2306.05479](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05479v1); [Oxford ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:03367c56-81e6-4fea-b51e-18885e3f6680/files/spc289k91q); [arXiv 2512.05734](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.05734)
- Censoring is the core statistical issue: many orders are cancelled or expire unfilled, so their true fill time is unobserved. A 20 s time-in-force is right-censoring at 20 s. — [KANFormer](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.05734)

### Inferences
- **Minimal practical fill model for a 20 s bid.** Let Q = estimated contracts ahead (from the ProbQueueModel tracker). Let D(20s) = contracts that will leave the queue ahead of you in 20 s, made up of sell-taker volume printed at your price plus cancels ahead of you. Then P(fill in 20s) ≈ P(D > Q). D can be modelled as compound Poisson / negative binomial using the recent taker-sell rate at the bid and the recent per-contract cancel hazard at that level. A sweep that trades *through* your price is also a fill, and by far the most toxic kind. That is the CST / Cont–de Larrard logic reduced to one level. A logistic or discrete-time hazard model (fill within [0,5),[5,10),[10,20) s, with censoring) on features {log Q, Q/level_size, taker-sell rate, cancel rate, opposite queue size, imbalance, spread, time-to-expiry, price level} is an easy, data-efficient choice for small samples. Deep survival models (MMW, Arroyo et al.) are likely overkill until there are many thousands of labelled orders.
- **Calibrate the cancel-split exponent on your own orders.** You control real orders and see when each one fills. For every live order, log the tracker's estimated Q over time and the actual fill time, then pick the prob-function family and exponent n that best predicts real fill times (likelihood of observed fill / no-fill at 20 s). This is the single biggest backtest accuracy lever on an L2-only venue, and it directly tests whether "250 ahead" is really 250.
- **Why 250 ahead is the break-even.** In a 1-tick-spread large-tick book, the gross edge per round trip is at most 1c. Moallemi–Yuan's result, that queue value and adverse selection both get worse further back in the queue, means orders at the back mostly fill when an informed or sweeping flow is about to move the price. The fix is structural: get to the front of the queue (join new or thin levels early, keep standing orders rather than re-posting every 20 s, which resets you to the back). Better prediction alone will not fix it.
- **20 s cancel-and-repost is costly.** Each repost throws away accumulated queue priority. The queue-value literature implies a scalper should keep orders resting while the signal is benign and cancel only when the signal turns toxic.

- Could not fetch the HLR, CST, Cont–de Larrard or Lo–MacKinlay–Zhang full texts (network block), so specific fitted numbers (intensity shapes, survival parameters) are not quoted beyond what is marked [TK].
- No published study was found that validates L2 probabilistic queue estimators against ground-truth L3 positions with reported error statistics. hftbacktest offers the models without published accuracy numbers.
- No empirical cancel-hazard estimates were found for prediction-market books.

## 2. Short-horizon predictors from the book (OFI, multi-level OFI, queue imbalance, microprice, trade-flow imbalance) and large-tick vs small-tick transfer

### Takeaway
In a large-tick regime (a 1c tick on a 0–100c contract with a spread usually pinned at 1 tick), **queue imbalance at the best levels is the most predictive and cheapest feature** for the direction of the next mid move. Gould & Bonart find fitted P(up) of about 0.8–0.9 at extreme imbalance for large-tick stocks, against only 0.6–0.7 for small-tick stocks. OFI (Cont–Kukanov–Stoikov) explains about 65% of *contemporaneous* price-change variance, and its slope is inversely proportional to depth; it is mainly a measure of the current pressure, with weaker forward predictability. The microprice (Stoikov) turns imbalance and spread into a martingale "fair price" and is a natural target or feature. Predictive horizons are a few price changes (seconds to minutes in equities). Cross-asset predictability fades within minutes.

### Cited Findings
- **OFI.** Cont, Kukanov & Stoikov (2014, J. Financial Econometrics 12(1):47–88; arXiv 1011.6402), 50 US stocks. Price changes over short intervals are driven mainly by order flow imbalance at the best bid and ask (limit orders + market orders − cancels, signed by side). The relation is linear, with slope inversely proportional to market depth. Average R² is about 65%. Trade imbalance alone explains much less. — [arXiv 1011.6402](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.6402); [Kukanov slides](https://www.smallake.kr/wp-content/uploads/2016/05/Kukanov.pdf); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:jfinec:v:12:y:2014:i:1:p:47-88.)
- **Caveat:** the CKS R² is contemporaneous (OFI and Δprice over the same window). It is not a forecast. — [Towards Data Science summary](https://towardsdatascience.com/price-impact-of-order-book-imbalance-in-cryptocurrency-markets-bf39695246f6/) (secondary; it states ~70%, the slides state 65%)
- **[TK] OFI definition** used by CKS, per event n: e_n = 1{Pb_n ≥ Pb_{n−1}}·qb_n − 1{Pb_n ≤ Pb_{n−1}}·qb_{n−1} − 1{Pa_n ≤ Pa_{n−1}}·qa_n + 1{Pa_n ≥ Pa_{n−1}}·qa_{n−1}. OFI over a window = Σ e_n. It is computable directly from an L2 delta feed. — [arXiv 1011.6402](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.6402)
- **Multi-level OFI.** Xu, Gould & Howison (2018, Market Microstructure and Liquidity): a vector OFI over several levels; out-of-sample fit of mid-price changes improves as levels are added. — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Multi-Level-Order-Flow-Imbalance-in-a-Limit-Order-Xu-Gould/4136bb7a6854685fe1e47c3e0c9e07dbcdc2d23d); [Oxford ORA](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:9b7d0422-4ef1-48e7-a2d4-4eaa8a0a7ec1/files/m89dedb16194e627a2c92d14e3329bd48)
- **Integrated OFI.** Cont, Cucuringu & Zhang (Quantitative Finance 23(10), 2023): the first principal component of multi-level OFIs explains >80% of their variance. The integrated OFI beats best-level and multi-level OFI in- and out-of-sample. Cross-asset OFI adds forecasting power only at short horizons (up to several minutes), and it decays quickly. — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/14697688.2023.2236159); [arXiv 2112.13213](https://arxiv.org/html/2112.13213v4)
- **Queue imbalance.** Gould & Bonart (2016, Market Microstructure and Liquidity; arXiv 1512.03492): I = (qb − qa)/(qb + qa), logistic regression of next mid-move direction on I, 10 liquid Nasdaq stocks. The coefficient is significant at 99% for all 10. Large-tick stocks show a "considerable improvement" over the null in binary and probabilistic classification; small-tick stocks only a "moderate" one. At I near 1, fitted P(up) ≈ 0.8–0.9 for large-tick stocks versus 0.6–0.7 for small-tick stocks. For small-tick stocks the fits "only slightly outperform the null model" on mean squared residuals. Local logistic regression is slightly better. — [arXiv 1512.03492](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.03492); [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/1512.03492v1); [SSRN 2702117](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2702117)
- Secondary sources say the binary-classification gain for large-tick stocks is about 50–60%, but this could not be verified in the primary text. Later work confirms that volume imbalance loses predictive power when the tick is small. — [Gould–Bonart search summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/1512.03492)
- **Microprice.** Stoikov (2018, Quantitative Finance; SSRN 2970694): the micro-price adjusts the mid by spread and imbalance and is built to be a martingale. It predicts short-term prices better than the mid or the volume-weighted mid. **[TK]** Estimation: discretize imbalance (e.g., ~10 buckets) and spread (1–2 ticks), estimate a Markov chain of (I, S) and mid changes, and solve for the adjustment g(I,S) as a limit of iterated expected mid changes. In the paper it converges after a few price moves. The test assets were BAC (large-tick) and CVX (small-tick). — [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Micro-Price:-A-High-Frequency-Estimator-of-Stoikov/791b497158d3e5eb5f6ad934e205bd637ca409ec); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327410053_The_micro-price_a_high-frequency_estimator_of_future_prices)
- The microprice estimator has to be re-estimated across tick-size changes (a 2025 EFMA study drops asset-days with tick changes and discretizes imbalance into deciles). A 2025 arXiv "Boltzmann price" paper revisits fair price for large-tick stocks whose mid moves in half-tick steps. — [EFMA 2025](http://www.efmaefm.org/0EFMAMEETINGS/EFMA%20ANNUAL%20MEETINGS/2025-Greece/papers/Efficient%20or%20not_Price%20measures%20in%20market%20microstructure.pdf); [arXiv 2507.09734](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.09734)
- **A related continuous fundamental price** on the discrete grid for large-tick assets: arXiv 1608.00756. — [arXiv 1608.00756](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1608.00756)
- **Large-tick theory.** Dayri & Rosenbaum (2015, Market Microstructure and Liquidity; arXiv 1207.6325): for assets whose spread is almost always one tick, the effective spread carries no information. They define an "implicit spread" that plays its role, and use it to define an optimal tick size and forecast the effects of tick changes (validated in arXiv 1507.07052). — [arXiv 1207.6325](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.6325); [HAL](https://hal.science/hal-01263181); [arXiv 1507.07052](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1507.07052)
- **[TK]** Eisler, Bouchaud & Kockelkoren (2012, Quantitative Finance), "The price impact of order book events": for large-tick stocks, queue volumes at the best quotes carry most of the information and prices move mostly through queue depletion. In small-tick stocks, in-spread limit orders and cancels matter more. The same theme appears in Bouchaud, Bonart, Donier & Gould, *Trades, Quotes and Prices* (CUP, 2018), on large- vs small-tick dynamics and queue imbalance. — no URL verified this session

### Inferences
- **Transfer to a 0–100c binary with a 1c tick.** In relative terms, the tick is 1/p of the price: 2% at 50c, 10% at 10c, 100% at 1c. That is far larger than any equity in these studies, so the market is extremely large-tick, especially in the tails. The literature implies (i) mid moves happen almost only by depletion of the best queue, (ii) queue imbalance at L1 should be *more* predictive than in equities, and (iii) the most important signal for a resting bid is "is the bid queue about to deplete?" Low bid size relative to ask size, plus sell-taker bursts, means the price is about to fall through you.
- **Thin books add noise.** Equity results use thousands of events per day. In a thin prediction market, single large orders dominate the imbalance, and spoof-like or quote-flicker behaviour may matter. Use size-capped or log transforms (e.g., I computed on min(q, cap) or log1p sizes) and include an "age of level" feature.
- **Bounded price changes the dynamics near 0 and 100.** Near the bounds the payoff probability, rather than a random-walk mid, governs drift, and the relative tick is huge. Imbalance models should be fit per price-band (e.g., 5–20c, 20–80c, 80–95c) or include price as an interaction.
- **Horizon.** The relevant horizon is the next mid change or 20–60 s, which matches the queue-imbalance and microprice horizons (a few price changes). Longer-horizon OFI is weak.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that tests OFI, queue imbalance or microprice specifically on prediction-market (Kalshi/Polymarket) books.
- Exact out-of-sample accuracy numbers for the microprice and for Gould–Bonart classification gains could not be verified from primary texts this session.
- Eisler–Bouchaud–Kockelkoren and Bouchaud et al. (2018) were not retrieved; their claims are [TK].

## 3. Adverse selection and markouts for maker fills; how market-making models handle it

### Takeaway
Measure adverse selection with **fill-conditional markouts**: for every maker fill, record the signed mid (or microprice) change at fixed horizons (e.g., 1, 5, 10, 30, 60, 300 s, and to settlement for binaries), averaged per fill and broken down by queue position at fill time, time-to-fill and fill type. A negative maker markout means the fill was adversely selected. The resting-order "winner's curse" shows up empirically: fast fills and back-of-queue fills are worse. Theoretical market-making models (Glosten–Milgrom; Avellaneda–Stoikov; Guéant–Lehalle–Fernandez-Tapia; Cartea–Jaimungal–Penalva) treat fills as Poisson with intensity decreasing in quote distance. They handle adverse selection either through the spread (GM) or with explicit drift/impact terms and signal-dependent quoting (CJP).

### Cited Findings
- A markout is the difference between the fill price and a later mid-price. It is the standard execution-quality measure and is also called the realized spread; with the usual sign convention, a negative maker markout means adverse selection. — [IEX SEC comment letter](https://www.sec.gov/comments/s7-05-18/s70518-4406380-175663.pdf); [Multi-level MM with RL, arXiv 2608.18195](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.18195); [Markouts blog](https://uditsamani.com/markouts/)
- Practitioner evidence (blog, not peer-reviewed): limit orders filled within 1 minute had negative average fill quality (−0.31), while those filled after more than 10 minutes were positive (+0.43) — "fast fills are bad fills". — [aligrithm post](https://aligrithm.com/adverse-selection-is-adverse-selection-porting-fast-fills-are-bad-fills-to-fx-and-futures/)
- Back-of-queue orders face more adverse selection; contracts ahead is linked to fills against larger and more informed trades (J. Financial Markets 2025). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1386418125000229)
- Moallemi–Yuan: the adverse-selection cost of a resting order rises with its queue position, which creates an arms race for the front of the queue. — [MIT seminar abstract](https://web.mit.edu/orc/www/archive/seminars/2014s/abs/Moallemi_abs.html); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2996221)
- Market simulation under adverse selection (arXiv 2409.12721) argues that backtests ignoring the correlation between fills and subsequent price moves overstate maker P&L. — [IDEAS 2409.12721](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/2409.12721.html)
- Limit-order placement with adverse selection risk and latency (arXiv 1610.00261); latency and liquidity provision in a LOB (Bonart & Gould, arXiv 1511.04116). — [arXiv 1610.00261](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.00261); [arXiv 1511.04116](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1511.04116)
- **[TK] Glosten–Milgrom (1985, JFE):** a competitive, risk-neutral dealer facing a share of informed traders sets bid = E[V | sell], ask = E[V | buy]. The spread exists purely to cover adverse selection, and quotes update in a Bayesian way after each trade. For a binary contract with V ∈ {0,1}, this maps exactly onto the setting: the fair bid is P(YES | a sell arrives). — [Rosu lecture notes on information in LOBs](https://people.hec.edu/rosu/wp-content/uploads/sites/43/2020/03/info_lob.pdf) (related survey)
- **[TK] Avellaneda & Stoikov (2008, Quantitative Finance):** mid follows Brownian motion with volatility σ; fill intensity at quote distance δ is λ(δ) = A·e^{−kδ}; CARA utility with risk aversion γ. Reservation price r = s − qγσ²(T−t); optimal total spread = γσ²(T−t) + (2/γ)ln(1+γ/k). Inventory skews quotes; adverse selection is *not* modelled (fills are independent of future price moves). — [arXiv/SSRN record not fetched]
- **[TK] Guéant, Lehalle & Fernandez-Tapia (2013, Math. Financial Economics):** solves the A–S problem with inventory limits and gives closed-form asymptotic quotes, δ*(q) ≈ (1/γ)ln(1+γ/k) + ((2q+1)/2)·sqrt(γσ²/(2Ak)·(1+γ/k)^{1+k/γ}). It still relies on exponential fill intensity and does not model adverse selection.
- **[TK] Cartea, Jaimungal & Penalva (2015, CUP), *Algorithmic and High-Frequency Trading*:** market-making chapters add (i) adverse selection, where a market order shifts the mid by a jump (impact of informed flow), (ii) short-term alpha / order-flow drift, and (iii) running inventory penalties. Optimal quotes widen or withdraw on the side where informed flow is expected. Cartea, Jaimungal & Ricci (2014, SIAM J. Fin. Math.), "Buy low, sell high: a high frequency trading perspective", models self-exciting order flow with adverse-selection jumps.
- Feil & Nendel (arXiv 2607.17991) frame prediction-market MM as balancing mark-to-market inventory risk against *settlement* risk; see section 4. — [arXiv 2607.17991](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.17991v1)

### Inferences
- **Correct markout protocol for this system.**
  1. For each maker buy fill at price p and time t, compute m(Δ) = mid(t+Δ) − p, and also microprice(t+Δ) − p, for Δ ∈ {1, 5, 15, 30, 60, 300 s}. Because the strategy exits by offering at p+1c, also record the realized outcome of the exit order: filled at +1c, cancelled and scratched at p, or stopped out at p−k. With a 1c tick, mid(t) − p right after a fill is often +0.5c if the spread is 1c. Report "mid at fill − p" separately so the spread capture is not counted as alpha.
  2. Break markouts down by estimated queue-ahead at entry, time-to-fill, whether the fill came with a price move through your level (sweep) or a trade at your price leaving the level standing, taker trade size, imbalance at fill, and price band.
  3. Compare against unconditional mid changes over the same horizons to isolate the selection effect: E[Δmid | your fill] − E[Δmid | random time at the same book state].
  4. For binaries, add a **settlement markout**, (1{YES} − p), for positions carried to expiry.
- **Winner's curse in the 1c-tick setting.** A resting bid at the best bid fills when a seller hits it. If the queue ahead is large, by the time you are reached the seller has eaten through Q contracts, so the selling is large and persistent: the bid is likely to deplete and the mid to drop by 1c. Your +1c offer then sits at what is now 2c above the new bid, and you hold a losing position. A fill-probability model trained without markout labels will maximize exactly these toxic fills. **Model EV per order = P(fill)·E[markout_exit | fill, features] − P(fill)·costs**, with the two parts estimated separately (two-stage, or a joint model with a fill-conditional head).
- Glosten–Milgrom maps cleanly onto 0/1 contracts: when an informed share is present, the "fair" bid sits below the mid by the expected information content of a sell. If the book is one tick wide, quoting at the touch is profitable only for fills where that information content is < 0.5–1c. Those are the front-of-queue fills, or fills from small, isolated, noise-like taker prints.

## 4. Market making in binary / prediction markets specifically

### Takeaway
A small but growing (mostly 2025–2026, not yet peer-reviewed) literature exists. The key structural differences from equity MM: (i) price is bounded in [0,1] and the terminal value is 0 or 1, so inventory risk is **settlement variance q²·p(1−p)**, largest at 50c and concentrated near resolution; (ii) inventory cannot be hedged until settlement; (iii) near expiry, flow becomes more informed and makers absorb it at unfavourable prices. For a 20 s scalper this mainly means: avoid quoting into resolution windows and news moments, and condition every model on time-to-expiry and price band.

### Cited Findings
- Feil & Nendel (arXiv 2607.17991, 2026), "Optimal market making in prediction markets": a stochastic-control setup choosing bid/ask to maximize expected terminal wealth while controlling mark-to-market inventory risk and settlement risk of positions open at resolution. They prove a unique optimal quoting strategy. The terminal penalty uses the conditional variance of settlement value, ∝ q²p(1−p): largest near 50%, shrinking as the outcome becomes near-certain. — [arXiv 2607.17991 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.17991v1); [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.17991)
- "Toward Black–Scholes for prediction markets: a unified kernel" (arXiv 2510.15205): inventory risk is concentrated near resolution and cannot be hedged by an underlying until settlement. — [arXiv 2510.15205](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.15205)
- Polymarket-v1 database paper (arXiv 2606.04217): Kyle's λ estimates are mostly *negative* on Polymarket. The authors put forward a "resolution-pressure" mechanism, where makers absorb flow at unfavourable prices near expiry, as a hypothesis. — [arXiv 2606.04217](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.04217v1)
- Sports prediction markets (arXiv 2607.14430): probability-weighting (Prelec) distortion is not constant but emerges sharply as expiry approaches. — [arXiv 2607.14430](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.14430v1)
- Other relevant preprints: settlement manipulation in prediction markets (arXiv 2606.31675); resolution-aware perpetuals on Polymarket data (arXiv 2605.10400). — [2606.31675](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.31675); [2605.10400](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.10400)
- **[TK]** Earlier theory: Hanson's LMSR (2003/2007) automated market makers for prediction markets (bounded-loss cost-function makers). Wolfers & Zitzewitz (2004, JEP), "Prediction markets", document the favourite–longshot bias. Both are background, not CLOB microstructure.

### Inferences
- In a 0–100c book, fill toxicity and imbalance predictiveness should depend strongly on (price band × time-to-expiry × event state, e.g., live game vs pre-game). Every model and feature should be conditioned on these, or trained per regime.
- The p(1−p) settlement-variance term means a scalper carrying un-exited inventory is most exposed near 50c, and a 1c tick is relatively cheapest there (2%). The tails (≤10c, ≥90c) have huge relative ticks and lower settlement variance, but carry longshot-bias and jump risk at resolution.
- Negative Kyle λ (if confirmed) suggests mean reversion after flow in some regimes, which would favour passive liquidity provision. This is preprint-level evidence only.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed empirical study of CLOB queue dynamics, fill rates or maker markouts on Kalshi or Polymarket was found. The papers above are 2025–2026 arXiv preprints and were read only via abstracts and snippets.
- Kalshi-specific microstructure (e.g., YES/NO book netting) was out of scope or not found.

## 5. Practical takeaways: features, fill model, and realistic backtesting

### Takeaway
To turn the scalper profitable, attack queue position first (join early, don't re-post every 20 s, skip levels whose expected 20 s depletion is much smaller than the queue ahead). Then gate entries with a fill-conditional toxicity model driven by L1 queue imbalance, depletion speed and taker-flow features. Backtest with a calibrated ProbQueueModel, bracketed by RiskAverse (pessimistic) and an optimistic bound, and validate the calibration against live fills.

### Cited Findings
- Conservative baseline: RiskAverseQueueModel advances queue position only on trades at your price. ProbQueueModel splits cancels ahead or behind with power or log functions; trade-through or best-ask-cross counts as a fill. — [hftbacktest docs](https://github.com/nkaz001/hftbacktest/blob/master/docs/order_fill.rst); [queue.rs](https://github.com/nkaz001/hftbacktest/blob/master/hftbacktest/src/backtest/models/queue.rs)
- hftbacktest warns that replay backtests cannot alter the market. Liquidity-taking orders are filled fully at the best regardless of size, which can be unrealistic, so live comparison is required. — [hftbacktest docs](https://github.com/nkaz001/hftbacktest/blob/master/docs/order_fill.rst)
- Queue imbalance predicts the next mid move strongly in large-tick books (P(up) ≈ 0.8–0.9 at extreme imbalance). — [Gould & Bonart, arXiv 1512.03492](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.03492)
- OFI slope is inversely proportional to depth, so in thin books each contract of flow moves price more. — [CKS, arXiv 1011.6402](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.6402)
- Queue value is about the size of the (half-)spread in large-tick books, and adverse selection increases with queue position. — [Moallemi & Yuan, SSRN 2996221](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2996221)
- Survival models with right-censoring are the correct framing for time-to-fill. — [Arroyo et al., arXiv 2306.05479](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05479v1)

### Inferences (ranked feature list, 5–10 features)
1. **Estimated contracts ahead Q** (ProbQueueModel tracker), plus **Q / level size** (relative position). This is the strongest fill and toxicity driver.
2. **Expected 20 s depletion of the queue ahead**: recent sell-taker volume at the bid per second (from trade prints with taker side) × 20 s, plus recent cancel hazard × Q. Use the ratio **Q / E[depletion]** as a single "time-to-front" feature.
3. **L1 queue imbalance** I = (qb − qa)/(qb + qa), with size-capped or log variants. A low I (thin bid relative to ask) predicts a downward move; skip or cancel bids when I is strongly negative.
4. **Short-window OFI** at L1 (and integrated over L1–L3) over the last 1–10 s, normalized by depth.
5. **Trade-flow imbalance**: signed taker volume over the last 5–30 s and the size of the last print relative to the bid queue. Large aggressive sells relative to the queue are toxic.
6. **Microprice − mid** (Stoikov-style, estimated per price band). Positive means fills at the bid are less toxic.
7. **Spread state**: 1 tick vs >1 tick. When spread > 1c, joining a new level gives near-front priority, a key queue-position opportunity.
8. **Level age / time since the best-bid level formed** and **whether you joined at level creation**. This proxies queue priority quality.
9. **Price band and time-to-expiry / event phase** (regime conditioning, per section 4).
10. **Recent volatility / mid-change rate** (ticks per minute). High rates mean more sweep risk and a lower chance the +1c exit fills.

### Inferences (fill model and decision rule)
- **Two-head model.** Head A is a discrete-time hazard / logistic model for P(fill within 20 s) with censoring. Head B is E[exit P&L | fill], the realized outcome of the +1c exit, or a 30–60 s markout if not exited. Post or keep the order only if P(fill)·E[P&L | fill] > threshold. Train Head B *only* on fills, with features measured at fill time *and* at entry.
- **Queue-position policy.** Prefer (a) posting immediately when a new best-bid level appears (spread widens to 2c, or the bid improves); (b) keeping orders resting across 20 s windows while features are benign, since cancelling resets priority; (c) skipping levels with Q above the empirically break-even Q (currently ~250), calibrated per price band.
- **Realistic backtest recipe.**
  1. Replay snapshots plus deltas in exact sequence. Insert your order at the back of the queue (Q = level qty at arrival time, *after* adding your feed and order-entry latency).
  2. Advance Q on trade prints at your price with the matching taker side. Allocate non-trade decreases using ProbQueueModel. Run at least three variants: RiskAverse (cancels never ahead of you), power n=2 or 3, and log, giving pessimistic, middle and optimistic bounds. Increases go behind you.
  3. Fill on trade-through or best-opposite-cross, and on Q ≤ 0 with a trade at your price. Model partial fills by remaining trade quantity.
  4. Watch for double counting: delta feeds often show the trade's level decrease as a separate delta. Subtract trade volume from the next decrease, as hftbacktest does with `cum_trade_qty`.
  5. Calibrate the cancel-split function on live orders: compare predicted and actual fill times and fill rates, and choose the variant whose fill rate and fill-conditional markout match live. If live P&L sits between the RiskAverse and Prob bounds, the backtest is credible.
  6. Report strategy P&L under the pessimistic model as the go/no-go criterion. Optimistic-model profits alone are not evidence.

### Gaps
- The ranking of features is inferred from equity and futures evidence plus large-tick theory. It has not been validated on prediction-market data in any found source.
- No found source quantifies how much queue-position tracking error (L2 inference vs truth) biases backtest P&L for maker strategies.
